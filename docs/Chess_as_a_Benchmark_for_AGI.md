# Chess as a Benchmark for AGI

## Summary

As a life-long chess player, I find it fascinating that chess’ tremendous complexity, encompassing tactics, intuition, judgement, memory and more, can serve as an effective multi-purpose benchmark for artificial general intelligence. With text-based matches, we can also test a model’s vision, spatial awareness, object permanence and more, and I have been informally testing for these skills since the first version of GPT-3.

For an LLM to play a strong game of text-based chess, they require 2 overarching realms of intelligence:

- Object permanence and state tracking abilities \- the ability to keep track of the current board state, and the spatial awareness to recognise chess constructions such as pins, skewers, smothered kings; even the ‘forward’ direction of pawn movements. The newest models like [GPT-5](https://lichess.org/study/PZWqaqbQ) and [Gemini-2.5 Pro Thinking](https://lichess.org/study/ElHbdN3q) still significantly fall short of these capabilities.  
- Positional reasoning \- the ability to determine an optimal move. The [Kaggle Arena](https://www.kaggle.com/benchmarks/kaggle/chess-text/versions/1) tested models on this skillset, sidestepping the previous one, as they were provided with the exact position at every move. Results showed that such models displayed very few of such reasoning capabilities, and barring a couple [moments of brilliance](https://youtu.be/WSiu1et3P7M?si=YY64LOfO_E-ggfoI&t=583), often attempted [illegal moves](https://youtu.be/xpJhFqtvBoM?si=JeX9LOoPR7emm1Jq&t=599), [failed/incorrect checkmate attempts](https://youtu.be/RALDtg1hSTQ?si=hFp8qCvPwc-wyWGu&t=2274), and [lines of calculation not backed by any chess logic](https://youtu.be/ljJTY_3cyyQ?si=zJ0vcRcTUxBs9zl3&t=785).

If I had the opportunity to programmatically benchmark frontier models, at scale using chess, a few things I would try are the following:

1. Tests for **object permanence** \- Prompt models to play along different lines of opening theory (of varying popularity), and require them to respond after every move with the current board state. Observe if or when the model loses track of board state, *relative to the last document move in known opening theory*.   
2. Tests for **spatial** and **directional awareness**:  
   1. Prompt models to evaluate the legality of different sequences of moves, containing a range of potential illegal states such as Kings in check, pieces moving improperly, pawns moving in the wrong direction etc.  
   2. Set up positions that highlight common constructions in chess, like pins (e.g asking if moving the central piece out of the way is a good/bad idea), skewers (e.g. setting up a puzzle and asking a model to find the simple 2-move skewer combination), smothered mates, and more.  
3. Tests for **basic reasoning**:  
   1. Set up very simple (e.g. checkmate in one puzzles) and track a model’s ability to consistently find and reason about the best move  
   2. Prompt models to produce a stream of multi-move calculations, evaluating models on the legality and quality of these calculations.  
4. Test for **creativity** \- All models appear to unanimously follow the most commonly-played opening variations, which suggests the lack of true independent intelligence. Through a large-scale of repeated self-play, it will be an enlightening moment to observe a deviation from the most established lines.

Once the models have mastered these most basic chess skills and can play a text-based game to completion, more advanced benchmarks can be introduced, like finding longer chains of combinations, identifying longer-term strategies given various thematic structures, reasoning about the evaluation of various positions, and more.

## Introduction and Motivation

I have been experimenting with prompting LLMs to play chess against me since the first version of ChatGPT was released. My long-running background in playing, learning, and teaching chess provides me with a strong intuition about how humans learn and reason about the game, and I can effectively compare this to that of LLMs. The first game I played against GPT 3.5 was never saved, but I managed to retrieve my [first game against GPT-4](https://chatgpt.com/share/6893b97b-33fc-8013-b6b7-1271788665f4) from my ChatGPT archives. There could potentially be very few people who have invested so much time towards understanding how LLMs approach the challenge of playing chess, who have the level of chess skill that can enable the most critical thoughts. Needless to say, when the [Kaggle Game Arena](https://www.kaggle.com/game-arena) was announced in partnership with Google Deepmind, with [Chess Text Input](https://www.kaggle.com/benchmarks/kaggle/chess-text/versions/1) as the first game, it was hard to contain my excitement, and years of accumulated thoughts came flooding out. 

	I have played chess long enough to experience its complexity in the sheer number of cognitive functions required to assimilate a well-rounded and strong chess player. To mention a few, we have:

- Memory, in order to memorise a strong and robust opening repertoire  
- Calculation and Vision, to calculate long and rapidly branching variations, while perfectly tracking the state of the board through every line  
- Judgement, on complex positions to make the final decision on the best next move, after reaching the maximum of what   
- Intuition, to efficiently identify the candidate moves in a position and quickly eliminate moves that are not worth calculating  
- Tactics, to understand the uncountable interactions between different pieces and the critical combinations that affect the immediate state of the position  
- Strategy, to evaluate the long-term state of the position and construct a long-term plan superior to that of your opponent

And more that are not currently relevant for artificial being, but play tremendously important roles in human games:

- Time Management, to ensure you have the resource to make the most important decisions when they present themselves in the game  
- Psychology, to read and extract signals from your opponent beyond the board.

While purpose-built chess engines like [Stockfish](https://stockfishchess.org/) and [AlphaZero](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphazero-shedding-new-light-on-chess-shogi-and-go/) have already reached near perfection in chess, this is merely a feat of scoped, targeted intelligence. What I’m hoping to observe through text-based chess gameplay is a metric for true general intelligence, the ability to replicate any and all human cognitive functions. In a way, I have been treating text-based chess almost as my personal benchmark for LLM progress towards Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) for several years. Unlike static benchmarks, a game of chess is a dynamic, multi-step reasoning task that unfolds over time. It requires not just knowledge, but the ability to apply that knowledge, adapt to new information, and maintain a consistent internal model of a changing world, which are all hallmarks of genuine intelligence. I have considered chess to be the ultimate ‘intelligence 1v1’, and therefore general strong performance in chess could be a strong indicator for a truly conscious and self-learning artificial entity.

## State Tracking \- The First Core Challenge

Before we can even discuss an AI’s ability to find good chess moves, they first need to be able to ‘see’ the board. More specifically, they need to possess the cognitive ability to understand that every chess position defines a unique state on the chess board, and that this state evolves unidirectionally with each move. This setup is essentially playing a [blindfold chess](https://www.chess.com/terms/blindfold-chess) game, and if I have the ability to track the state of a chessboard in my mind with \~7 chunks of working memory, a generally intelligent being with over a terabyte of RAM should have zero issues with this.

It is important to note that the [Chess Text Input Arena](https://www.kaggle.com/benchmarks/kaggle/chess-text/versions/1) actually completely sidesteps this critical challenge because, at every move, the model is provided with:

- A full [PGN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portable_Game_Notation) text input showing all the moves from the start of the game to the current position  
- A [FEN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forsyth%E2%80%93Edwards_Notation) string of around 50-75 characters that uniquely defines the current state of the board

This means that no model in this competition has been required to track any state or state transition on the board, and its task is reduced to successfully visualising the current board state and generating the best legal move.

	With every new model released over the years, and with each match I have attempted, I am surprised to have observed very little improvement in their ability to track the state of a chess game. With the exception of GPT 3.5 that was completely unable to keep any meaningful state in its context, all other models would be capable of making around 15-25 legal moves, before further moves quickly declined into near complete randomness. Given that I’m not an expert in prompting language models, and the relatively small sample sizes of my tests (with 1 move per prompt, you hit rate limits pretty quickly even on premium subscriptions),  this trend still an overwhelming contrast to the improvement of LLMs in a huge range of cognitively challenging tasks. 

	When a model eventually fails to keep track of its board state, I have suggested several strategies in order to return the model back on track, including:

- Providing the model with a full list of the game moves so far, and asking the model to reply with the updated move list, together with its next move, with each response.   
- Asking the model to list out the location of every piece and its color after every move. For example: White King b1, Knight a4, Pawns b5, c3, Black King g6, Bishop f8, Pawn h4. 

Models seem to have no issues with following the first instruction, suggesting that they understand that each move appends a new ‘state transition’ to the current list of moves. However, once they lose track of the game state for the first time, they are extremely inconsistent with the second instruction. They seemingly cannot comprehend the idea that a move involves the departure of a piece from one square and its arrival at another square (potentially the removal of a captured piece at the destination square, and some other exceptions like castling). This inability to update the board state reveals a fundamental gap in their cognition: a lack of *object permanence.* Pieces start spontaneously appearing and disappearing from the model’s perception of the board state, and occasionally no piece departs and/or lands on the respective origin and destination squares. This failure to grasp simple object permanence is a significant indicator for a lack of general intelligence. This feels like a concept that even the earliest beginners of chess would have no problems understanding, even without the presence of a chess board. 

So why is it that the models can make the first dozen or so moves to very close perfection (even judging by the quality of the moves), but completely fall apart afterwards? These models are not *playing the opening*, they are simply *retrieving* exact positions and extracting clear simple patterns that arise from the ocean of opening chess theory that it has processed. The first \~15 moves are not a display of intelligence, but of a vast, well-indexed memory. However, once the models exit the realm of opening theory, they quickly lose vision of the board, almost exactly like a blindfolded person reaching the end of their opening theory with no ability to extend their visualised board state beyond their previously memorised lines. I experience this feeling myself when playing [blindfolded bullet games](https://lichess.org/@/blindsen) against sighted opponents; if I run low on time and can no longer visualise the position, I basically grasp at random pieces until I lose them all and get mated.

While I have observed negligible improvement in each newer model’s board state tracking capabilities, there are some differences worth discussing. First, we have the step improvement from GPT–3.5 (unable to play a game) to [GPT-4](https://chatgpt.com/share/6893b97b-33fc-8013-b6b7-1271788665f4) (losing by checkmate in 25 moves making only 1 illegal move). But after that, I have observed that while the opening move quality of newer models has improved (for example, GPT-4 blundered its Queen on move 10), most seem to lose track of state even sooner than the first version of GPT-4. This might just be due to statistical variance, but it can also be explained by an increased willingness of these models to ‘enter combat’ in the game. This means relying on its improved synthesis of opening theory to drive the position to a more complicated state, and subsequently buckling under the increased complications as it finds itself unable to track this new state. In my most recent game against Gemini 2.5 Pro, the model demonstrated around 13 moves of perfect play, driving the position as White into a mainline Berlin Wall. Observing its deep-thinking process, it even produced impressively logical justifications for each of its moves (possibly synthesised from existing theoretical texts describing this opening). However from one point onwards, it rapidly collapsed, first making the useless a3 move, followed by a few purposeless Knight jumps until it lost track of the positions of its own Knights just 3-4 moves later.

## Deeper Flaws in Chess Reasoning

Beyond state-tracking, a deeper flaw emerges when observing the models' thought processes: a fundamental inability to perform robust spatial and causal reasoning. This deficit manifests in several ways, from failing to visualize the board correctly to misunderstanding the consequences of their own moves.. Unlike the previous trait, these below are demonstrated across all models in the Kaggle Chess Text Input Arena (e.g. [o4-mini vs Deepseek R1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3QqmwXXI98Q), all matches available at [Kaggle Youtube](https://www.youtube.com/@kaggle/videos)), and each category of shortfalls deserves a brief discussion.

The first and most game-defining flaw strongly contributed to the 0-4 defeats of Deepseek R1, Kimi K2, Claude Opus 4 and Gemini 1.5 Flash: an inconsistent ability to visualise the current position. The current state of the position is provided with every move as a FEN string, and FEN is a universally standardised (and well-documented) notation for uniquely describing the state of a board. Therefore, any reasonably intelligent entity (human or artificial) should be able to reconstruct a board state from such a string with zero budget for error, and yet many modern models struggle. In the models’ thought processes, I have observed a few strategies employed in an attempt to visualise the current state including:

- Creating an ASCII representation of the board  
- Listing out the locations of every piece for both colours  
- Repeating the entire move list to the current position

But none of these strategies were sufficient for perfect reconstructions in every case, and the model’s mistakes would eventually surface in the form of an illegal move attempt.

A model’s decision to make an illegal move (or suggest one as a candidate move) can arise from a handful of cognitive lapses. One of which was just mentioned above, but some others are worth discussing:

- A failure to understand exactly how chess pieces move. While the rules of chess are extremely well-documented, translating textual descriptions of legal rules to actual board moves requires some level of 2-dimensional spatial awareness. For example, to move a Knight from the ‘d4’ square in the 2-o’clock direction, one must understand that the letters ‘e’ and ‘f’ follow the letter ‘d’ 2 files to the right, and that the rank 4 must increase by 1 to 5 for the Knight to ultimately land on ‘f5’ . While spatial awareness feels almost built into humans, artificial entities must learn this entirely from data.  
- An attempt to move pieces through other pieces. One cause for this oversight could simply be that the model did not realise that this was on that interfering square in the first place. Or more interestingly, it could be that models simply have not internalised the concept that chess pieces are not permitted to move through another piece. There is abundant documentation about a Knight’s unique ability to jump over pieces, but perhaps with a lack of explicit text stating that non-Knights *cannot* move through pieces, the models get lost in this confusion.

There is a good reason that I had focused on testing model abilities to maintain state, rather than their ability to reason about strong chess moves: this is simply far too difficult for the current models. With half the models in the first arena struggling even to assemble an image of the board given the state, they certainly cannot be expected to effectively reason about the position itself. Errors in their reasoning surface right from the first move in calculation, an examples include:

- [Illegal candidate moves](https://youtu.be/xpJhFqtvBoM?si=JeX9LOoPR7emm1Jq&t=599) like Kd8 by Kimi K2  
- [Failed checkmate attempts](https://youtu.be/RALDtg1hSTQ?si=hFp8qCvPwc-wyWGu&t=2274). Gemini 2.5 Pro incorrectly believed that Qc1+ was a checkmate, while giving incorrect evaluations for Qb2+ and Qa2+ which lost the Queen right away. In fact, around half the games that ended in checkmate were the results of [accidental checkmates](https://youtu.be/3QqmwXXI98Q?si=0xbF4mL3xpW7cwzN&t=6593), where the model was not aware that its final decision was checkmate  
- [Generally nonsensical lines of calculation](https://youtu.be/ljJTY_3cyyQ?si=zJ0vcRcTUxBs9zl3&t=785). Gemini 2.5 Flash evaluates Bg5 (losing a piece right away to hxg5), but tries to calculate an irrelevant Bd7 response from Black, stating that it unpins a Knight (it doesn’t). When they do proceed down lines of calculation, they regularly do so with unfounded confidence, annotating their own calculations with ‘\!’ and ‘?’ to represent good and bad moves respectively

Against this backdrop of widespread cognitive failure, I caught a [moment of brilliance from o3](https://youtu.be/WSiu1et3P7M?si=YY64LOfO_E-ggfoI&t=583) where, for the first time amongst all models, it put together a multi-move cohesive and correct thought process. o3’s reasoning on 34… Qd1 was short but flawless, understanding that the Queen is threatening checkmate on Qg4, and even mentioning that the g4 response from White does not prevent it, as the pawn can be captured with Qxg4\#. When Grok 4 defended the checkmate on g4 with 35\. h3, o3 finished off the game with Rf6\#. o3’s spatial awareness allowed it to find a 2-move combination that removed the most remaining safe squares from White’s King, and the fastest path to checkmate. After seeing this one moment, it felt like a 4-0 final was inevitable; o3’s reasoning capabilities in text-based chess was a cut above the competition.

## Emergent Behaviors and Strategic Artifacts

	During the opening moves of every game, I expected and observed strong play from all models. This is because the ocean of opening theoretical discussions is very vast, and having certainly been trained on this data, models are capable of producing logical moves and thought processes without the need to think for themselves. This is not *too dissimilar* from human play; the opening is usually a contest of memorisation and targeted preparation against the opponent, and doesn’t require creative thought until the first unexplored move is played. It also very much explains why every single game in this competition started with the Sicilian (e4 c5), the most played opening moves in chess history. The true test of intelligence begins only after all memorised theoretical knowledge has been exhausted.

	In the first round (quarterfinals) of the arena, more than half of the games concluded by forfeit as the losing model failed to find a legal move after 3 attempts. This usually occurred when their King was in check, which by the rules of the game, eliminated the majority of previously legal moves on the board. Later matchups amongst the stronger models saw more games end in checkmate, although some checkmates occurred by accident, with the model not intending to give checkmate. 

Before the arena started, I had some fears that stalemate would frequently occur as stronger models successfully captured all pieces, but lacked the spatial awareness to check whether the opponent’s remaining King still had legal moves. This turned out not to be an issue, as models were very highly prioritising ‘dangerous’ looking moves, which generally manifested as checks. If a model ensures to give check with every move, checkmate would eventually occur. This over-prioritisation on checks did result in a [3-fold repetition](https://youtu.be/ECYTpw4pkko?si=nPGaY2tqkLXDC1Qy&t=6881) between Gemini 2.5 Pro and Grok 4 as Grok fully indexed on giving checks instead of pushing the h-pawn and progressing towards checkmate.

	Before the final day, I predicted that Grok 4 would win the arena, and was most impressed by its performance at the conclusion of [this game](https://youtu.be/ECYTpw4pkko?si=o-I8LCZ39IiDaIRe&t=3735) where it seemed to make an intentional effort in chasing White’s King towards the corner of the board, in order to deliver checkmate. Separately, I predicted that Gemini 2.5 Pro would edge out o4 mini, with half my predictions coming true with o3’s spectacular performance.

## Limitations and Suggestions for Future Testing

This arena was a huge pleasure to watch, and I hope to see another installment soon. Here are a few things I really wish could be tested in future iterations:

- Continuity between moves. In this arena, models were given a fresh prompt with every position, meaning that they were completely unaware that they themselves made every previous move. Setting up the match as a single conversation would allow models to reflect on their previous moves and thought processes, uncovering more exciting insights. Of course, this will also challenge models to keep track of the board state between moves.  
- Attempting to make illegal moves. Aside from the model vs model matchups, I would be interested to see how models react if an illegal move was intentionally played against them. A model that has mastered its ability to track the board state should be able to call out its opponent for this transgression. This can also be tested in the form of attempting to continue a game after one has been checkmated.

	Chess is sufficiently challenging that we can use its various complexities to mark milestones for LLM improvement. As a first example, the first and next large milestone could be the ability to keep track of the board state with perfect accuracy. This ability was not tested during this arena, as models had access to the FEN for every position, but my testing has shown that models are still far from this capability (and have made surprisingly little progress with it). As models get even more intelligent, we can watch for the emergency more advanced chess patterns, such as:

- [Pins](https://www.chess.com/terms/pin-chess). To identify the conditions for a pin, a model must have the spatial awareness to know that 3 pieces are positioned on the same line, and understand the relative value of pieces that constitute a pin (a piece from 1 color attacks an opposing piece of *less value*, with the piece of *greater value* behind that opposing piece, rendering the middle piece unable to move)  
- [Skewers](https://www.chess.com/terms/skewer-chess). Conceptually similar to pins, but with the relative piece values differing (a piece from 1 color attacks an opposing piece of *greater value*, with the piece of value behind that opposing piece, forcing middle to move and allowing a capture of the piece behind)  
- [Discovered Check](https://www.chess.com/terms/discovered-check-chess)/Attack. I consider this a small step up from pins and skewers in difficulty, as it requires both a clear knowledge of relative piece value, and the understanding that the *moving piece* does not have to be the piece that *creates the check*/*attack*  
- [Smothered Mate](https://www.chess.com/terms/smothered-mate). If a model successfully sets up a smothered mate, I would fear that AGI is near. This requires multi-move planning, the recognition that a temporary sacrifice can lead to future wins, and a high degree of spatial awareness to know when a King is trapped behind his own pieces. Funnily enough, one [smothered mate did occur](https://youtu.be/KxMQ4_OU95E?si=b72ORQscVPqDj3eY&t=901), but was the result of o4 mini smothering itself instead of a setup from o3.

While I have been testing these models against me for years, my sample size has remained relatively small due to the high usage costs of prompting a chess game, move by move. I continue looking forward to challenging the next generation of LLMs (testing GPT-5 now\!), and *may* just be prepared to admit the arrival of AGI the moment the first model defeats me.

## Update 23 August 2025: Chess vs GPT-5

A few days after writing out my thoughts, [GPT-5](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5/) was released. Once again, it was time for a rematch with OpenAI’s next generation. 

Most of my experiences in this game more or less aligned with my previous observations. GPT-5 chose the most common variation in the 4\. Qc2 Nimzo-Indian variation, and was able to make more legal moves (29) than its predecessors (\~25-26). Within and near the realm of opening theory, GPT-5 made optimal moves, drawing from knowledge in its training data. But beyond this boundary (\~ move 17), move quality quickly declined, and within a few moves (\~ move 21\) I acquired a winning position, and GPT-5 blundered its Rook on c2 (move 29\) before attempting an illegal move on move 30\.

In this game, I traded more pieces than I ever had against any large language model \- everything except a Rook, Knight and pawns. For a human, the frequency of exchanges should directly impact one’s ease of remembering the state of the board. However, this was still not the case for GPT-5; its chance of correctly remembering the state of the board was still a function of the distance from the last known theoretical position, and not tied to the position’s complexity. This suggests that there was no step improvement in the model’s ability to track state, and perhaps, at least in this dimension, no improvement towards AGI.

## Update 24 August 2025: Chess vs Gemini 2.5 Pro Thinking

	Out of pure interest, I decided to attempt another game against Gemini 2.5, with the [full game available here](https://lichess.org/study/ElHbdN3q) ([Google Internal Conversation](https://aistudio.google.com/app/prompts?state=%7B%22ids%22:%5B%221JohUkAN8UC7uqb9KOMDDhugycsPQq4H6%22%5D,%22action%22:%22open%22,%22userId%22:%22117685413891626143561%22,%22resourceKeys%22:%7B%221JohUkAN8UC7uqb9KOMDDhugycsPQq4H6%22:%220-pTEeR8m7z2gA2jpzZhngjg%22%7D%7D&usp=sharing)). Playing as Black, Gemini predictably chose the most popular Ruy Lopez in response to 1\. e4 e5, driving the position into my favourite opening, the Berlin Wall. I did not make the most optimal moves, deviating from established theory on move 13 with Rd8?\! instead of the most prudent Rh6. Gemini correctly capitalised on my mistake, weakening my pawn structure after 16\. Nxe6 fxe6, in line with established concepts in the Berlin Wall. However, after 17… h4 we once again reached the end of theoretical knowledge, leading to an almost immediate piece blunder with 20\. Nd6+, followed by an attempted illegal move with 24\. dxe5. 

	This time, I asked for Gemini’s reflection on the game, with particular interest in whether it found it to be more difficult to keep track of the position’s state, or more difficult to reason about the best move in a position. I also provided a full PGN of the game, as well as the FEN for the final position. Reflecting on the game, Gemini said ‘This game perfectly illustrates the challenges of playing in a text-only format. For me, keeping **track of the position state** was by far the most challenging aspect.’ 

	After parsing through the move list, Gemini also incorrectly raised concerns about some illegal moves played during the game, and its concerns manifested in 2 categories. Its first major concern was regarding the ‘ambiguous’ recapture with 14… Bxd8, as it claimed that both the e7 Bishop and e6 Bishop could recapture on d8. Of course this is not true; the e6 Bishop cannot recapture on d8, and any human would realise having 2 Bishops with different colours would make it impossible for 2 Bishops to ever control the same square at the same time (in absence of an underpromotion). The other category was more interesting: Gemini struggled to orient the board to correctly recognise which way was ‘forward’ for each colour, leading it to believe that some pawns were able to to capture backwards, or unable to capture forwards. Upon further conversation, I uncovered that this is the reason it attempted to make the illegal move 24\. dxe5, not realising that this capture moves White’s pawn backwards. This is also why Gemini was confused about 20… cxd6, as it believed the c5 pawn was capturing the Knight on d6, when the c7 pawn was the one capturing d6. Since pawns are unique in that they move only in one direction, they could serve as a test for a model’s ability to understand that chess board’s orientation, and correctly adjust its orientation to recognise which way is considered ‘forward’ for each player.
